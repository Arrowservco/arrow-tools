Executive summary

What the three October audit datasets show, using only records dated October 1–30, 2026. Figures here are full-period and unfiltered. “Program” labels are used because the three files measure different things and are never added together.

Scope

  • 9,420 qualifying October records after removing 611 duplicates from 10,031 October-dated rows.
  • Program A – agent-action audits (reconnects placed / disconnects cancelled): 6,686 unique actions.
  • Program B – disconnect-order audits: 2,705 unique orders.
  • Program C – company error reports: 29 reports.

Key achievements

  • 90.4% of audited agent actions (5,726 of 6,336) were error-free.
  • 85.5% of Program A audits were logged the same calendar day as the action.
  • Program A error rate was 2.3% and stayed between 2.1% and 3.0% in every week.

Areas of concern

  • Gas-related OPCOs drive errors: EMA Gas (8.0%) and EGMA (4.2%) produced 103 of 148 errors (69.6%) from 32.0% of audits.
  • Very High priority disconnect orders carry a 10.9% error rate vs 2.8% for Medium High.
  • Audit completeness: 350 Program A records (37.0% of Cancelled Disconnect records) and 123 Program B orders have no audit result.
  • Follow-up not captured: only 35 of 115 Program B errors name an agent and 6 record coaching.

Recommended priorities

  1. Run a targeted review of EMA Gas and EGMA reconnect / cancel-disconnect handling.
  2. Add a pre-disconnect check for Very High priority orders.
  3. Close the audit-result and coaching documentation gaps, and stop duplicate audit records at intake.

Key insights

Ordered by business significance. “What happened” is observed fact; “Reason / caveat” is interpretation and is not established by these files.

Gas-related OPCOs account for most Program A errors

EMA Gas 8.0% · EGMA 4.2% vs EMA Electric 1.0% · WMA Electric 1.4%
What happened
EMA Gas (36 of 448 audited) and EGMA (67 of 1,582) together produced 103 of 148 errors (69.6%) while representing 32.0% of audited records. EMA Gas also has the highest coaching-only rate (10.7%).
Why it matters
Customers served under these OPCOs are far more likely to be affected by an agent-handling error, and each error can mean a wrongly timed disconnect or reconnect.
Reason / caveat
The files do not say why. Process differences, order mix or system behavior for these OPCOs are possible but untested here.
Action
Sample these OPCOs more heavily, review the error comments for common causes, and confirm whether a job aid or system check is missing.

Very High priority disconnect orders have nearly four times the error rate

10.9% (57 ÷ 524) vs 2.8% (58 ÷ 2,058)
What happened
Very High orders are 19.4% of Program B orders but 49.6% of its errors (57 of 115). Both groups have large samples, so the gap is not a small-sample effect.
Why it matters
These are the orders flagged at the highest priority, so mistakes are most consequential here.
Reason / caveat
Priority level may correlate with account situations that are harder to handle correctly; this is an observed association only.
Action
Add a mandatory pre-disconnect verification step for Very High orders and track their error rate weekly.

Program B error rate peaked in week 4 before easing

W2 4.0% · W3 4.0% · W4 6.0% · W5 4.3%
What happened
After a small first week (only 93 audited orders), weekly error rates were 4.0%, 4.0%, 6.0% and 4.3% (W5 is incomplete). Program A was flat by comparison (2.1%–3.0% across weeks).
Why it matters
A sustained increase would mean disconnect-order quality is not improving as audit coverage grows.
Reason / caveat
Week-to-week movement across four points could be noise or a change in which orders were loaded for audit; the data cannot separate these.
Action
Watch the next two weeks; if W4's level persists, review the 38 W4 error records for a shared cause.

Audit results are missing for a sizable share of records

A: 350 records · B: 123 orders
What happened
350 of 945 Program A Cancelled Disconnect records (37.0%) have no result, so Cancelled Disconnect rates rest on 595 records. All 123 Program B orders without a result are tagged “10/7/2026 Audit List Refresh”.
Why it matters
Rates exclude these records. If the missing audits would have found errors at the observed rate, true error counts are higher; if they are placeholders, nothing is lost. The files do not say which.
Reason / caveat
Unfinished audits or list-refresh placeholders.
Action
Confirm whether these are pending audits; complete or close them so the denominator is clean.

Cancelled Disconnect actions draw coaching far more often than reconnects

22.7% (135 ÷ 595) vs 5.7% (327 ÷ 5,741)
What happened
Coaching-only outcomes are about four times as common for Cancelled Disconnect audits, while their error rates are similar (2.0% vs 2.4%).
Why it matters
Coaching-only findings flag process deviations that did not (yet) become errors; a high rate signals a recurring handling gap for this action.
Reason / caveat
Cancelled Disconnect audits may be judged on different criteria than reconnect audits.
Action
Review the coaching-only comments for Cancelled Disconnect and consider a focused refresher.

Errors are spread across many agents, with a repeat group

71 of 118 audited agents had ≥1 error · 37 had ≥2
What happened
The 10 agents with the most errors account for 54 of 148 errors (36.5%); the highest single count is 11. Agent IDs are anonymized in this report.
Why it matters
Errors are not confined to a handful of people, so team-wide process fixes matter as well as individual coaching; the repeat group is the natural first coaching list.
Reason / caveat
Differences in call volume per agent are not available, so error counts are not rates.
Action
QA leads should pull the repeat-error list from the source file and prioritize those agents for coaching.

Follow-up details are rarely recorded for Program B errors

Agent named: 35 of 115 · coaching recorded: 6 of 115
What happened
Of 115 errors, 80 have no “Agent Responsible” and 109 have no “Coached” entry. 13 have no issue category.
Why it matters
Errors that cannot be tied to an agent or a coaching action cannot be closed out, and coaching effectiveness cannot be measured.
Reason / caveat
The fields may be filled elsewhere or only for some audit paths.
Action
Make Agent Responsible and Coached required whenever the result is Error Found or Coaching Only.

Duplicate audit records inflate raw counts, especially in Program B

A: 222 removed (3.2%) · B: 388 removed (12.5%)
What happened
Program B had 223 orders recorded more than once (388 extra rows; 44 orders had audited copies with different results). Program A had 222 repeated actions, 28 with conflicting results.
Why it matters
Without de-duplication, counts and rates would be overstated and some orders would carry two answers.
Reason / caveat
Overlapping audit-list refreshes and re-audits.
Action
Block duplicate Doc Number / action entries at intake and define which audit is the record of truth.

Date filtering

Rule applied to each file: date ≥ 2026-10-01 00:00:00 AND date < 2026-11-01 00:00:00. Timestamps were used exactly as recorded (the files carry no time-zone information, so no conversion was applied). Each file has several date columns, so the event date was chosen as the reporting date for all three because the report describes what happened in October; this is an assumption — the alternatives are quantified below.

SourceRows reviewedDated before Oct 1Dated Nov 1 or laterMissing / unreadable dateOctober rowsDuplicates removedAnalyzed (unique)
  • Program A – excluded for date: 19,262 dated Jul–Sep (Jul 193, Aug 8,392, Sep 10,677); 393 without a usable date: 17 fully blank rows and 376 rows whose timestamp was stored as a time fragment only (e.g. “48:35.2”) with no date. 141 of those 376 rows were created on Oct 15 and may be October activity; they are not included because their action date cannot be determined.
  • Program B – excluded for date: 884 orders with September activity dates (all were modified in October during audit; they are excluded because the activity itself was not in October) and 5 blank rows.
  • Program C – excluded for date: 1,076 reports with error dates from 2022-12-02 to 2026-09-30. None have missing or later dates.
  • Sensitivity to the date choice (raw rows, before de-duplication): Program A by record-created date = 7,153 (vs 6,908 by action date); Program B by last-modified date = 3,982 and by created date = 3,365 (vs 3,093); Program C by created date = 36 (vs 30). Different choices would change volumes by these amounts but would not change the direction of the findings reported here; they would, however, bring in non-October events, so they were not used.

Duplicate handling

  • Program A: same account + same action timestamp + same audit type is the same audited action. 222 such second records were removed (earliest record kept, preferring one that has an audit result). Re-audits that disagreed with the kept record: 28 of 222. 2 records lacked a usable account number and were kept as-is.
  • Program B: Disconnect Doc Number is the order key. 388 extra records for 223 orders were removed (preferring a record with an audit result, then the earliest). Of these, 44 orders had two or more audited copies with different results, and 107 had a copy with no result alongside an audited copy. Auditors also wrote “duplicate” in comments on 63 October records.
  • Program C: 1 record removed — same account, error date and error type, created 15 minutes apart with essentially the same description.
  • Across programs: the three files describe different events, so no cross-file de-duplication was applied and no cross-program totals are used for rates. 333 accounts appear in both A and B, 10 in A and C, and 1 in B and C; these are informational only.

KPI definitions

  • Unique records = October rows after duplicate removal. With result = records whose result is No Error Found, Coaching Only, Error Found- Before or Error Found - After. No result = blank result (never treated as “no error”).
  • Error rate = (Error Found- Before + Error Found - After) ÷ records with result. Coaching-only rate = Coaching Only ÷ records with result. The files do not define “Before” and “After”; they are reported as labeled.
  • Same-day audit rate (A) = records whose Created date equals the action date ÷ unique records.
  • Report-level counts: Program C “call pulled” and “review approved” are shares of all C reports (all have a value).

Data-quality observations

  • Program A: 350 October records have no audit result (all but one were created Oct 7–8 by one user and are Cancelled Disconnect records, so they look like unfinished or placeholder entries). One record has a created date 14 days before its action date. Account numbers appear with prefixes, spaces and 10-digit variants; they were normalized to digits only for duplicate matching and are not displayed.
  • Program B: all 123 records with no result carry the comment “10/7/2026 Audit List Refresh”. The “Auditor” field is blank on most rows and has spelling variants, so “Modified By” is used for workload. “Agent Responsible” and “Coached” are mostly blank even for errors (see Insights).
  • Program C: OPCO labels (CTE, EMA, EGMA, CTG, WME) differ from Programs A/B (EMA Electric, EGMA, WMA Electric, EMA Gas) and were not mapped to each other. “Account Fixed” is recorded for 1 of 29 reports, so a fix rate is not reported.
  • Fields not analyzed: free-text notes, payment dates (Last Speedpay / Last Tracked) and times, because their meaning is not defined in the files.
  • Privacy: no customer, account number, agent name or agent ID appears in this report. Auditor names appear only in workload tables.

Limitations

  • Partial month (see banner). Program A has no records for Oct 12; Oct 9, 13 and Fridays are lighter than other days. The files do not say why, and the report does not infer a reason.
  • The files show audit samples, not full populations; no target or benchmark was supplied, so nothing is rated against a goal and no other month is compared.
  • Differences between small groups (for example Program C or Program A groups with few audited records) should not be over-interpreted; counts are always shown beside rates.
